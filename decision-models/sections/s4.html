The 0.562 on the ambulance row is the model's own confidence in "Yes", at that position, for that prompt. It is the same weight that drives the sampling: if you ran that call a thousand times, "Yes" would come up about 562 times. That is the entire content of the number, and it is a fact about the model's internal state, not a fact about how often its answers turn out to be right.

So the question becomes: can I treat that confidence as the chance the answer is correct? The model's confidence in "Yes" and the truth of "Yes" are two separate facts, and nothing guarantees they agree. A model can be deeply confident and wrong, or timid and right. Calibration is the property that they do agree: across many calls, the calls where the model said 0.6 work out right about 60% of the time, the ones where it said 0.9 right about 90% of the time, and so on.

One thing the test above applies to is not just LLMs. Every rung emits probabilities, so every rung is checkable this way. The difference is what each rung does about the result. The readout hands you a probability and nothing else: the test is something you run yourself, once, on your labeled set, and if it fails you know the threshold you set on paper is not the threshold that is running. The trained heads are built to pass the test by construction: Jev's RLCD objective and Clef's published training loss both include a calibration term, Brier among them, so the model is trained to make stated probability match observed accuracy. That is the real headline difference between the rungs: on the readout, calibration is a property you measure and maintain; on a trained head, it is a property the training guarantees, and when a domain shifts you re-run the same test to find out when the guarantee expired.

the test: does what it claims match what happens

Take a set of calls you know the truth of. Sort them by the probability the model stated, and count how often the true answer was actually "Yes" in each group. If the model is honest, the observed column matches the claimed column.

what it claimed how often it was actually right

The threshold is why this is not academic. A cutoff only means what you think it means if the scale it sits on is honest. If the model is overconfident, your 0.6 cutoff admits calls it is really 0.52 sure about, and the bar you set on paper is not the bar that is running. If it is underconfident, your "escalate anything below 0.9" rule sends humans to calls the model had already settled at 0.97, and you pay for caution you do not need. Either way the failure is silent, because the text interface would have looked exactly the same.

The single-number summary of the test above is the Brier score, the mean squared distance between each stated probability and the 0/1 outcome:

Brier = mean over calls of ( pi − oi )²

where pi is the stated probability of the outcome that was true and oi is 1 if it happened. You compute it once, on your labeled set, before a threshold does work.

a second trap: the number belongs to the prompt that produced it

The next-token distribution is conditioned on the entire prompt, so the wording of the instruction and the exact label tokens are part of what is being scored. Same model, same conversation, different prompt:

question"Answer with Yes or No""A. Yes / B. No"
ambulance needs to know?0.5620.679
cardiologists need to know?0.9970.995
kitchen needs to know?0.0010.010

The confident rows barely move. The borderline ambulance row moves 0.562 to 0.679, which is the difference between below a 0.6 threshold and above it. Same model, same patient, different routing, purely from the wording. This is not noise to average away; it is the mechanism doing its job. The rule that follows: a threshold is only valid for the prompt it was measured on. Change the wording, the labels, or their order, and you must re-measure before trusting the cutoff.
