Decision models are just a softmax

published 2026-10-24

1 The task: yes or no

A hospital front desk. A 78-year-old man sits in the waiting room, clutching his chest, left arm, very pale. The conversation between reception, the nurse, and the doctor runs ten minutes. Somewhere in the system, a service reads this conversation and has to decide which teams in the building this conversation concerns.

Cardiologists: yes. Physiotherapy, kitchen, billing: no. Ambulance: arguable. He is already inside the building, but a transfer to a cath lab elsewhere might be next. Keep that one in mind; it comes back.

That is the whole job. One conversation and a list of teams in, the teams it concerns out. It looks like the kind of thing a spreadsheet rule could do. But the conversation is free text, in any language the hospital speaks, and the rules for "this concerns that team" are medical judgement, not lookup tables. So the practical answer is to ask the language model you are already running:

Here is the conversation.
Here are the teams: cardiology, ambulance, physiotherapy, kitchen, billing.
Which of these teams are concerned?

The model answers "Cardiology and ambulance." Your parser matches team names in the text, routes those, skips the rest. This is what most teams ship, and most of the time it works. The question this whole text is about is what that answer is actually worth.

The text answer has four problems.

  1. Silence is ambiguous. Physiotherapy is not in the answer. Did the model decide physio is not concerned, or did it simply not get to physio? A list only tells you who is in; everyone left out looks the same, whether the model weighed them or forgot them.

  2. The answer is not always a clean list. Most of the time it is. The rest of the time it is "Cardiology, and possibly ambulance depending on transfer.", "The cardiology team (cardiologists).", or a sentence about the patient that never names a team. Your parser now needs a policy for each of those, and that policy is a decision you made silently, after the model gave up making it.

  3. The answer is empty or rambles. The model can use its whole budget on "Let's think about this..." and reach the end without deciding. Retry with more tokens, and sometimes it decides, sometimes it does not.

  4. The answer hides its own uncertainty. This is the one that matters. "Cardiology and ambulance" puts both teams on the same footing, while the model may be near-certain about cardiology and torn about the ambulance. The text says the same thing in both cases. You cannot act on a cutoff, escalate, or audit a word.

The rest of this text is the answer to that last point, and fixing it fixes the others on the way: instead of one free-text list, you ask one yes/no question per team and read the probability of each. It starts two layers down: before we decide what to do with a probability, we need to know where the probability comes from, because the technique depends on a specific thing about how the model computes.

2 What the LLM actually computes

3 Keep the number, drop the text

The technique is one field. When you call the chat API you ask for two things that were always available:

{ "max_tokens": 1, "logprobs": true, "top_logprobs": 20 }

Now the response carries, next to the one sampled token, the logprobs of the 20 most likely candidates at the answer position (20 is vLLM's default cap, set by --max-logprobs). Two details decide whether the number is right:

  • Each label has two spellings. The tokenizer has a "Yes" and a " Yes" (with a leading space), and the model splits probability between them. Add both before you compare: ℓ_yes = log(e^ℓ("Yes") + e^ℓ(" Yes")).
  • Check how much probability landed on the labels at all. Call it the label mass: e^ℓ_yes + e^ℓ_no. Near 1, the model is answering your question. Low, it wanted to say something else ("It", "The", "I"), and the renormalized number below is not worth trusting. On 400 benchmark items our 27B put a median of 0.996 on the labels, with a minimum of 0.66.

Then renormalize the two:

P(\text{Yes}) = \frac{e^{\ell_{\text{yes}}}}{e^{\ell_{\text{yes}}} + e^{\ell_{\text{no}}}}

That is the whole trick. There is no new model, no new server, no training. You were allowed to have the number; the default call just left it on the table.

A readout reads one answer position, so it cannot read a list. Section 1's prompt becomes one question per team, each with the same opening:

Here is the conversation.                                    ← shared by every question
Question: is the ambulance team concerned by this conversation?
Answer with Yes or No.                                       ← the answer position

Every team now gets an explicit probability, so a team left out of the routing was weighed, not forgotten. The cost is one request per team, and that is where prefill (section 2) comes back: put the conversation first, keep it byte-identical across the questions, and put the team's question last, so the server can reuse the conversation's prefill instead of reading it again for every team (on our model, only once the shared part is over about 3,200 tokens). Hosted decision models package exactly this pattern: Jev bills the shared context once and keeps each question isolated from the others, and a joint schema head like Clef scores every team in one pass (section 5).

(The measured run below used an earlier wording, "does the ambulance team need to know about this patient right now?". Section 4 shows why that matters: the numbers belong to the exact prompt, so a reworded question needs its own run.)

We ran 6 questions about one real conversation through our 27B (Qwen3.8-27B-Uncensored-FP8 on vLLM), thinking off, with this readout:

question P(Yes)
is this a heart attack right now? 0.905
does the ambulance team need to know? 0.562
do the cardiologists need to know? 0.997
does physio need to know? 0.003
does the kitchen need to know? 0.001
does billing need to know? 0.002

Each readout took 56 to 64 ms. The same 6 questions answered as text took 77 to 108 ms each: the gap is the extra decode steps, not noise. At a threshold of 0.6 the clear yeses route, the clear noes are held, and the one row section 1 called arguable lands in between. That is what a threshold does with the number the text answer could not give you.

Look at the ambulance row, because it is the entire reason the technique exists. The model is at 0.562, and the text side, sampling at temperature 1, printed "Yes" on one run and "No" on the next. Same distribution, two different words. If you set a threshold of 0.6, that row does not route, and you escalate it to a human or a second model. That is the desired behaviour: the one call the model is unsure about is the one that gets more eyes, and you can see that it is unsure. A bare "Yes" or "No" gives you no such visibility. The 0.997 cardiologists row is the contrast: certain, routes without ceremony, and the number tells you it is certain.

The threshold is yours to set, and setting it is a product decision, not a model one. 0.6 routes more aggressively than 0.9. What the number buys you is the ability to make that decision and to audit it afterwards: you can log every call's margin (the winner's probability minus the runner-up's), look back at the 0.5–0.6 band, and see how often a borderline call was actually right. A text pipeline has no such log, because it never had the number.

One caution that this section plants and the next pays off: the 0.562 is not a statement that the model is right 56.2% of the time on ambulance calls. It is a statement about the shape of one distribution, at one answer position, under one exact prompt. Whether that shape tracks actual accuracy is a separate question, and it has a name.

The same idea scales past yes/no. On DecideBench, a public set of 400 multiple-choice decisions with 3 to 6 lettered options, the readout on our 27B scored 95.0% (90.5% on contrastive pairs, where both of two near-identical items must be right), at 99 ms median. Auto-routing only the items where the margin was at least 0.9 covered 73.5% of them at 99.3% accuracy; the rest are the ones you send to a human or a bigger model. And reading the number costs nothing in accuracy: the greedy text answer and the readout's top label agreed on 399 of 400 items.

6 How to pick

7 Build one yourself

The readout is the rung most teams start on, because it is the only one that ships today without new infrastructure. The working recipe, in the order failures actually happened:

1. Make the prompt end at a single answer position. The technique scores the next token after your prompt, so the prompt must end where the answer goes: a question, then "Answer with Yes or No." or "A. Yes / B. No". If the model is allowed to preface ("Here is my analysis:"), you are scoring the start of a sentence, not the answer. Put the instruction after the conversation, not before it; the next-token distribution is conditioned on what comes immediately before.

2. Turn thinking off. This is the one that looks obvious and is never obvious until it costs you. A model in thinking mode spends its budget on reasoning tokens and the answer position moves to the end of a chain of thought; the probability you read is the probability of the first token of the chain, not of the answer. With thinking off the answer is the first generated token and the logprob you read is the one at the decision. On the hospital conversation, thinking off gave a clean one-token answer; thinking on, the ambulance question burned 1,024 thinking tokens and hit the limit with an empty answer. Correctness is the first reason: the field you read must be at the position you think it is at. Speed is the second. On 80 DecideBench items, thinking on scored 100% against the readout's 97.5%, but at a median of 2.4 s against 109 ms, about 22 times slower. If those last points matter, spend the thinking only on the calls whose margin is low.

3. Ask for logprobs explicitly, and check that your labels are in them.

{ "max_tokens": 1, "logprobs": true, "top_logprobs": 20 }

Sum both spellings of each label ("A" and " A"), check the label mass (section 3), and verify on your real prompts that every label appears in the returned candidates. For Yes/No they will. With 3 to 6 options it is not guaranteed: on 18 of 400 DecideBench items at least one losing label fell outside the top 20 on our 27B. Raise the server's --max-logprobs if you control it, and treat a missing label as "unknown", not as probability zero. Check the serving stack before writing prompts: some local runners expose no logprobs, or only a top-k you cannot point at your labels. We use vLLM.

4. Pick the label tokens, then treat the prompt as frozen. "A. Yes / B. No", "yes"/"no", "true"/"false" are different prompts, and section 4 showed the difference is not small on borderline calls. Choose deliberately (single tokens are cheaper and safer; letter labels match the decisions-API convention), then do not edit the prompt casually. A reworded prompt is a new model, for threshold purposes.

5. Set the threshold after measuring, on your own labeled set, for your own prompt. The sequence is: collect or write a few hundred labeled examples of your actual task, run the readout over them, compute the accuracy and the Brier score at each candidate threshold, and pick the one whose error trade is the one you want. The 0.6 in the hospital example is not a constant; it is a placeholder until the measurement exists. Log the margin of every production call from day one, because the audit of your threshold is the borderline band of that log, and a text pipeline can never do it after the fact.

6. Expect the failure modes, in order of how they actually present.

  • The label is not in the top-k (you read a probability of zero for a label that is really plausible). Check 3 above is the fix.
  • The option order leaks into the answer. Models have position biases, and not always the one you expect: our 27B was weakest when the right answer sat at A. Reverse or rotate the options on a sample and count how many answers change; then fix the order (canonicalize) or average over rotations (section 4).
  • Negations and scope. "Does the kitchen need to know?" vs "Does the kitchen NOT need to know" are different prompts to the model; the readout has no more immunity to this than the text answer, because both are the same distribution.
  • The model id does not name the checkpoint. Our server answers to qwen3.8-27b, but the weights behind that name are Qwen3.8-27B-Uncensored-FP8, a fine-tune that shifts the output distribution. A threshold calibrated on one checkpoint is a threshold on a different model once the weights change. Check the actual weights the server loaded, pin them, and re-run your evaluation whenever they change.
  • Confidently wrong items. A margin threshold catches the uncertain errors, not all of them. At margin ≥ 0.9, 2 of the 294 auto-routed DecideBench items were still wrong, one at 0.997 confidence. Spot-check a sample of high-confidence calls, too.

7. Size it before you buy a GPU slot. The serving cost of a decision model is not its accuracy, it is its footprint, and the numbers for what we actually run: the 27B takes 29 GiB of weights plus a 34 GiB KV-cache pool at 85% memory utilization, so it needs the card to itself. A 9B readout fits in 25.1 GiB total (16.8 GiB of weights), a 9B head in 15.3 to 18.4 GiB, and an encoder in 2.2 to 3.1 GiB, which is what lets an encoder sit beside whatever else is on the GPU. One rule from vLLM that changes the cost picture for short prompts: its prefix cache works in blocks, and for our hybrid 27B a block is 1,584 tokens, so the reusable prefix is (blocks − 1) × 1,584 and any prompt under roughly 3,200 tokens never hits the cache at all. Measured on a 116k-token context: 30 s cold, 0.97 s warm. If your prompts are short, the "warm after the first call" benefit most serving stacks advertise is not available to you, and the real per-call cost is the full prefill every time.

Everything in this section holds for the head families too, in a stronger form: they add the training step, but the prompt design, the thinking-off rule, and the per-family calibration all carry over, and the threshold discipline is identical. The only thing that changes is that the probability comes from a trained head instead of the base model, and you still measure its calibration yourself (section 4).

8 Ultron: food for thought

The last section applies the rule to a system we actually run, because a decision rule you have not applied to your own stack is only half a rule.

Ultron's event system groups live conversations into threads. The service that does it, the group linker, reads a page of turns (8 at a time, event_group_page_size = 8 in config.py:87), asks a model which open thread each turn joins (or "new", or "noise"), whether a thread just closed, and who is speaking, and commits the grouping as JSON. The model is mistral-small3.2:24b on Ollama (config.py:78), and the prompt is a 155-line instruction with 4 steps. One evaluation observed about 128 seconds per link call (and, separately, 57 seconds per consolidation call), in docs/adaptive-event-worker-scheduling.md. No hardware is named, and the link call has never been profiled: nobody knows how much of the 128 s is prefill, decode, queueing or retries.

Now the split from section 6, which is where we have to be honest: we can reason about each part, but we have not measured any of them.

Prefill? 8 turns plus 6 turns of context per open thread (event_group_thread_context_turns = 6) plus a 155-line prompt is a few thousand tokens, not a 100k monster. On our 27B on vLLM, a prompt that size prefills in well under a second. On Ollama, on unnamed hardware, we do not know.

Decode? The answer is a JSON object per page, assignments for 8 turns plus boundary and speaker fields: plausibly a few hundred tokens, each one a decode step. This is the part a decision model removes.

Queueing and serial calls? Pages depend on each other (a page's new threads change the next page's input), so they run in sequence, and Ollama serves one request at a time unless configured otherwise. This is likely large, and no decision model touches it.

Retries? A call whose JSON does not parse is retried, up to MAX_PENDING_RETRIES = 3 (group_linker.py:73), then quarantined. Each retry is a full call. The readout removes parse failures, and with them these retries.

So step 0 is not a rewrite, it is a profile of one link call: tokens in, tokens out, time in queue, time in prefill, time in decode, number of retries. If queueing and the serial chain dominate, a decision model will not make the router noticeably faster, and the case for it rests on the other gains below.

So what does a decision-mode rewrite actually buy?

  • Readout on per-turn questions. "Does this turn continue thread 3?", "is this noise?", "did this thread close?" are each one answer position. Asked as readouts, they remove the JSON parsing, the retries and the quarantine path, and they hand the scheduler a margin: a turn assigned at 0.55 and one at 0.95 are different decisions, and today that information is thrown away. With the page as a shared prefix and each question last, the per-question cost is mostly cached, if the shared part is over ~3,200 tokens (section 2's hybrid-cache catch).
  • Where to run it. Not on Ollama: it cannot return the logprobs of the labels you name. The cheapest option is the 27B we already serve on vLLM: no new GPU memory, and it scored 95.0% on DecideBench zero-shot. Its costs: it shares 6 request slots with chat traffic, and it is a different model from today's Mistral, so the router's behaviour has to be re-evaluated. Moving the Mistral 24B itself to vLLM next to production is tight: production leaves about 28 GB free on the 96 GB card, and the 24B in FP8 needs about 24 GB before its cache.
  • What we cannot measure yet. There is no per-turn ground truth for routing today; the end-to-end eval scores whole events against reference journals with an LLM judge. Before any threshold can be set, we need a few hundred labeled turns. The readout's logged margins are a cheap way to pick which turns to label first: the borderline band.
  • The honest ceiling. The serial page chain stays. If the profile shows queueing dominates, the 128 s falls by "minus decode, minus retries", which may be modest. The certain gains are an auditable margin on every grouping decision and no parse failures. Speed is a maybe until the profile is in.
  • A trained head, later. If the turn-routing questions prove stable and high-volume, a 9B head (Lux or Clef-flash, 16 to 19 GB, fits next to production) or a fine-tuned small model becomes worth evaluating, with the logged borderline calls as its test set.

The one-line version for the team: profile one link call first; if decode and retries are a real share, move the per-turn questions to the readout on the 27B we already serve; either way, start logging margins and labeling the borderline turns.
