Decision models are just a softmax

published 2026-10-24

1 The task: yes or no

A hospital front desk. A 78-year-old man sits in the waiting room, clutching his chest, left arm, very pale. The conversation between reception, the nurse, and the doctor runs ten minutes. Somewhere in the system, a service reads this conversation and has to answer one question for each team in the building:

Does this team need to know about this patient right now?

Cardiologists: yes. Ambulance: yes. Physiotherapy: no. Kitchen: no. Billing: no.

That is the whole job. One conversation in, a handful of yes-or-no answers out. It looks like the kind of thing a spreadsheet rule could do. But the conversation is free text, in any language the hospital speaks, and the rules for "this team needs to know" are medical judgement, not lookup tables. So the practical answer is to ask the language model you are already running:

Here is the conversation.
Question: does the cardiologist team need to know about this patient right now?
Answer with Yes or No.

The model answers "Yes." Your parser takes the first word, checks it is "Yes" or "No", saves it, moves to the next team. This is what most teams ship, and most of the time it works. The question this whole text is about is what the "Yes" is actually worth.

The parser above has a quiet assumption: the model's first word is a yes or a no. It is not, always. The model gives you "Yes" 90% of the time, and the other 10% is where the code you did not write starts living: "It depends on the symptoms.", "Yes, although I would also consider the physio team.", or a sentence about the patient that never answers the question. Your parser now needs a policy for what each of those means, and that policy is a decision you made, silently, after the model already gave up making it. 2. The answer is empty or rambles. The model can use its whole budget on "Let's think about this..." and reach the end without deciding. Retry with more tokens, and sometimes it decides, sometimes it does not. 3. The answer hides its own uncertainty. This is the one that matters. A "Yes" from the model is at the cardiologists row and a "Yes" at the ambulance row, but the model is near-certain about one and genuinely torn about the other, and the text says exactly the same thing in both cases. You cannot act on a cutoff, escalate, or audit a word.

The rest of this text is the answer to that third point. It starts two layers down: before we decide what to do with a probability, we need to know where the probability comes from, because the technique depends on a specific thing about how the model computes.

2 What the LLM actually computes

3 Keep the number, drop the text

The technique is one field. When you call the chat API you ask for two things that were always available:

{ "logprobs": true, "top_logprobs": 10 }

Now the response carries, next to the sampled token, the logprob of the top ten candidates at the answer position. If your prompt ends with "Answer with Yes or No", the ten most likely candidates almost always include "Yes" and "No" with their logprobs, and you turn them into a probability of yes by inverting the log and renormalizing the two:

P(\text{Yes}) = \frac{e^{\ell_{\text{yes}}}}{e^{\ell_{\text{yes}}} + e^{\ell_{\text{no}}}}

That is the whole trick. There is no new model, no new server, no training. You were allowed to have the number; the default call just left it on the table.

We ran the six teams on a real conversation through a 27B model, thinking off, with this readout, and measured both the probability and the latency:

team P(Yes) time
heart attack right now? 0.905 63 ms
ambulance needs to know? 0.562 54 ms
cardiologists need to know? 0.997 53 ms
physio needs to know? 0.003 54 ms
kitchen needs to know? 0.001 56 ms
billing needs to know? 0.002 56 ms

A threshold of 0.6 routes exactly the right teams: cardiologists and heart attack through, everything else held. That is what a threshold does with the number the text answer could not give you.

Look at the ambulance row, because it is the entire reason the technique exists. The model is at 0.562. If you set a threshold of 0.6, that row does not route, and you escalate it to a human or a second model. That is the desired behaviour: the one call the model is unsure about is the one that gets more eyes, and you can see that it is unsure. A bare "Yes" or "No" gives you no such visibility. The 0.997 cardiologists row is the contrast: certain, routes without ceremony, and the number tells you it is certain.

The threshold is yours to set, and setting it is a product decision, not a model one. 0.6 routes more aggressively than 0.9. What the number buys you is the ability to make that decision and to audit it afterwards: you can log every call's margin, look back at the 0.5–0.6 band, and see how often a borderline call was actually right. A text pipeline has no such log, because it never had the number.

One caution that this section plants and the next pays off: the 0.562 is not a statement that the model is right 56.2% of the time on ambulance calls. It is a statement about the shape of one distribution, at one answer position, under one exact prompt. Whether that shape tracks actual accuracy is a separate question, and it has a name.

6 How to pick

Five rungs, five different trades. The selection rule is not "which is the best decision model", it is "what is the thing that is actually hurting you". Name the bottleneck first, then the rung follows:

bottleneck the rung that addresses it what you are actually buying
nothing hurts, you want visibility logprob readout (rung 1) the margin, for thresholds and an audit log; cost: one field in the request
accuracy is below what you need, serving stack is fixed fine-tuned base or trained head (rungs 2-3) a higher accuracy at the same speed and context; cost: a training set you now own
latency or cost per call, in volume encoder (rung 4) 100-300x throughput; cost: 512-token context, per-domain retraining, no generative output
you need long context AND open labels AND no decode candidate head (rung 5) 16k context with free-text labels, one pass; cost: a second model to version and serve
confidence is the problem, accuracy is fine stay on rung 1, fix calibration (section 4) a threshold that means what you think it means

Two things the table does not say by itself.

The first is that the bottlenecks are not exclusive, and the rungs do not compete for the same slot the way people frame it. A system can be on the readout and run an encoder for its high-volume, fixed-label path: the encoder takes the 60% of calls that are "which of these nine intents", and the LLM readout takes the rest, including the long-tail and the borderline ones the encoder's margin flagged. That split is the shape of a production system that has hit real volume, and it is cheaper than pushing either tool outside its strength.

The second is that the table assumes you have measured your bottleneck. "Our router is slow" is not a bottleneck, it is a symptom. The measurement that decides the row is the decomposition of the slow call: if it is prefill (long prompt), you need a smaller model or a shorter prompt, and no decision-model rung helps. If it is the serial pipeline (many sequential calls, each short), you need batching or parallelism, and the decision model changes what you call in each one. If it is decode (long rambly answers), then the readout or a head genuinely removes it, because the answer becomes one token or no tokens at all. Do that decomposition before picking a rung, or you will buy the wrong thing and it will feel like decision models do not work.

The Ultron section applies exactly this: it names the measured bottleneck, decomposes it, and shows which rung the system is on today and which one the numbers actually support moving to.

7 Build one yourself

The readout is the rung most teams start on, because it is the only one that ships today without new infrastructure. The working recipe, in the order failures actually happened:

1. Make the prompt end at a single answer position. The technique scores the next token after your prompt, so the prompt must end where the answer goes: a question, then "Answer with Yes or No." or "A. Yes / B. No". If the model is allowed to preface ("Here is my analysis:"), you are scoring the start of a sentence, not the answer. Put the instruction after the conversation, not before it; the next-token distribution is conditioned on what comes immediately before.

2. Turn thinking off. This is the one that looks obvious and is never obvious until it costs you. A model in thinking mode spends its budget on reasoning tokens and the answer position moves to the end of a chain of thought; the probability you read is the probability of the first token of the chain, not of the answer. With thinking off the answer is the first generated token and the logprob you read is the one at the decision. On the hospital conversation, thinking off gave a clean one-token answer; thinking on, the ambulance question burned 1,024 thinking tokens and hit the limit with an empty answer. There is no speed argument for thinking off (section 3 showed it is fast either way); there is a correctness one: the field you read must be at the position you think it is at.

3. Ask for logprobs explicitly, and check that your labels are in them.

{ "logprobs": true, "top_logprobs": 10 }

Then verify, on your real prompts, that both labels appear in the returned top candidates. For Yes/No they will. For a multi-class task where one label is rare, it may not, and your readout silently degrades to "label not in the top-10", which you should handle as "unknown" rather than as a probability of zero. This is the serving-stack wall in general: several popular local runners either do not expose logprobs at all, or expose only a top-k you cannot direct at your labels. Check the serving stack before the prompt, because the stack decision is the one that is expensive to reverse.

4. Pick the label tokens, then treat the prompt as frozen. "A. Yes / B. No", "yes"/"no", "true"/"false" are different prompts, and section 4 showed the difference is not small on borderline calls. Choose deliberately (single tokens are cheaper and safer; letter labels match the decisions-API convention), then do not edit the prompt casually. A reworded prompt is a new model, for threshold purposes.

5. Set the threshold after measuring, on your own labeled set, for your own prompt. The sequence is: collect or write a few hundred labeled examples of your actual task, run the readout over them, compute the accuracy and the Brier score at each candidate threshold, and pick the one whose error trade is the one you want. The 0.6 in the hospital example is not a constant; it is a placeholder until the measurement exists. Log the margin of every production call from day one, because the audit of your threshold is the borderline band of that log, and a text pipeline can never do it after the fact.

6. Expect the failure modes, in order of how they actually present.

  • The label is not in the top-k (you read a probability of zero for a label that is really plausible). Check 3 above is the fix.
  • The prompt leaks a position bias. Some models lean on the first option; if your "Yes" is always option A and your yes-rate is suspiciously high, swap the order on a sample and see if the margin inverts.
  • Negations and scope. "Does the kitchen need to know?" vs "Does the kitchen NOT need to know" are different prompts to the model; the readout has no more immunity to this than the text answer, because both are the same distribution.
  • The checkpoint moved under you. Same API id, different weights on the other end, and your calibrated threshold is a threshold on a different model. Pin the checkpoint, and re-run your evaluation when it changes. This bit us mid-measurement: the komodo server swapped FP8 checkpoints under an identical model id, and the borderline number moved with it.

Everything in this section holds for the head families too, in a stronger form: they add the training step, but the prompt design, the thinking-off rule, and the per-family calibration all carry over, and the threshold discipline is identical. The only thing that changes is that the probability you calibrate is a trained quantity with a published objective behind it, rather than a base model's belief that you are measuring after the fact.

8 Ultron: food for thought

The last section applies the rule to a system we actually run, because a decision rule you have not applied to your own stack is only half a rule.

Ultron's event system groups live conversations into threads. The service that does it, the group linker, reads a page of turns (8 at a time, event_group_page_size = 8 in config.py:87), asks a model what each turn joins, and commits the grouping. The model is mistral-small3.2:24b running on Ollama (config.py:78), and the prompt is a 155-line instruction with four steps: parse the page, match turns to existing threads, decide boundaries, emit a JSON structure. One observed evaluation measured the full pipeline at 128 seconds per link call, 57 seconds of that in model time (the number is from an eval trace in docs/adaptive-event-worker-scheduling.md, no hardware named, so treat it as a measurement of that run, not a constant of the system).

Now the decomposition from section 6, because it decides everything:

Is it prefill? Partially. 8 turns plus 6 turns of context per open thread (event_group_thread_context_turns = 6) is a real prompt, but it is not a 32k-context monster. A 24B model can prefill it in under a second. Not the bottleneck.

Is it the serial pipeline? Mostly, and this is where the 128 seconds go. The linker works page by page, and a page's output (new threads, boundaries) changes the next page's input, so the pages cannot trivially be parallelized: it is N sequential model calls plus retry rounds (MAX_PENDING_RETRIES = 3 in group_linker.py:73). 57 seconds of model time across a sequence of calls, plus round-trips to a local Ollama that serializes requests, plus the retry rounds that fire when a call's JSON does not parse. The pipeline is the bottleneck, and a decision model does not remove a serial pipeline by itself.

Is it decode? This is the one the decision model actually touches. The current output is a JSON object per page: thread assignments, a boundary flag, a speaker name. It is short, but it is generated and parsed, which means every one of the naive failure modes from section 1 is live here: a missing field, a stray word inside a number, an empty answer that costs a full retry round of up to 3. The retry and isolation machinery in the linker (calls that fail a parse get held back and retried) exists to catch failures that a non-generating call does not have.

So what does a decision-mode rewrite actually buy, rung by rung?

  • Rung 1 (readout) on the per-turn questions. The decomposable part of the linker's job is a set of yes/no and pick-one questions: "does this turn continue thread 3?", "is this a speaker boundary?", "is this noise?" Each of those is one answer position. Reading the model's logprobs on them removes the JSON parse, the retry rounds, and the quarantine path for exactly those calls, and it hands the scheduler the margin: a turn assigned to a thread at 0.55 is a different routing decision than one at 0.95, and today that information is discarded along with the rest of the decoded text. The serial page structure stays, because that is a data-dependency problem, not a decode problem.
  • What it costs. The prerequisite is the serving stack: Ollama's logprob API cannot name your tokens, and the feature request for it (logprob_tokens, issue #18580) was closed unmerged in favor of a higher top-k, so the readout on Ollama is top-k-only and the label-is-missing failure mode from section 7 is live. The move is vLLM, which the decision API exists for and which the komodo stack already runs. That is the expensive-to-reverse decision, and it comes first, before any prompt is written.
  • The honest ceiling. Even with a perfect readout, the pipeline stays serial and the 57 seconds of model time does not fall by 100x; it falls by "minus the decode, minus the retries", which on a short-answer task is real but modest. The 128 seconds does not become 2 seconds. What it does become is a pipeline where every grouping decision is logged with its margin, the borderline band is auditable, and the failure modes that require a retry round no longer exist. That is the gain, stated without the speed claim: confidence and measurability, not latency.
  • What a trained head could add, later. If the per-turn questions prove stable (they are: the thread/boundary/noise taxonomy does not churn), they are exactly the fixed-label, high-volume shape where an encoder or a candidate head earns its retraining cost, and the readout's margins become the evaluation set for free: every logged borderline call is a labeled example of where the small model must at least tie the big one. That is the split from section 6, the encoder takes the stable 60%, the readout takes the long tail.

The one line version, for the team: the router's pain is the serial pipeline and the decode-retry loop; decision mode removes the second one and measures the first, and the ticket to do it is serving the 24B (or its successor) on vLLM.
